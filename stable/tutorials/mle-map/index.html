    Fitting hyperparameters¶

    The lengthscale and measurement variance affect how a GP explains our data. A short lengthscale allows rapid changes in the function. A large measurement variance allows more of the variation in the observations to be attributed to measurement error. How can we choose these settings from data, and when does prior knowledge change the answer?

    We'll fit a GP to fifteen noisy measurements of a sine function. We know the underlying function in this synthetic example, so we can compare our predictions with it. The fitting procedure sees only the measurements.

    Choose what to fit¶

    We'll fit the RBF lengthscale and the measurement-error variance shared by all fifteen observations. The kernel variance stays at 1.0, giving function values a prior scale of order one.

    Wrapping a value in PositiveParameter makes it trainable and keeps it positive. Ordinary numbers and arrays stay fixed. The initial values below are starting points for optimization, not constraints on the fitted values.

    For the MAP fit, suppose previous experiments suggest a lengthscale around 0.5, and instrument calibration suggests a measurement variance around 0.02. We'll express this knowledge using log-normal priors. Each prior's log_mean is the logarithm of its median, and log_standard_deviation controls its spread. These choices leave room for the data to change our estimates:

    Quantity Prior median Approximate central 95% prior range
    Lengthscale, in location units 0.5 0.25–1.0
    Measurement variance, in squared function-value units 0.02 0.0075–0.053

    MAP requires a prior for every explicit Parameter. We'll attach them now, but the likelihood-only fit will ignore them.

    Fit by MLE and MAP¶

    The short initial lengthscale lets the mean follow local fluctuations, while the interval leaves considerable uncertainty between the observations and outside their range. We'll now let the data inform these settings.

    Maximum marginal likelihood (MLE) chooses hyperparameters that make the observed values most probable after averaging over the unknown GP function. Maximum a posteriori estimation (MAP) also uses the parameter priors we specified above. It favors settings that explain the data and have high prior density.

    fit_lbfgs minimizes an objective, so we supply the negative log marginal likelihood for MLE and the negative log posterior for MAP. Both fits use the same starting model.

    In [7]:
    Copied! 
    from gpex.fitting import (
    fit_lbfgs,
    negative_log_marginal_likelihood,
    negative_log_posterior,
)


def mle_objective(candidate):
    candidate_gp, candidate_data = candidate
    return negative_log_marginal_likelihood(candidate_gp, candidate_data)


def map_objective(candidate):
    candidate_gp, candidate_data = candidate
    return negative_log_posterior(candidate_gp, candidate_data)

    Compare the fitted predictions¶

    Both fits increase the lengthscale and reduce the measurement variance. For this sample, MLE estimates a measurement variance of about 0.0053. MAP puts it closer to our calibration prior, at about 0.0099, and chooses a somewhat shorter lengthscale than MLE.

    The fitted means follow the broad rise and fall of the function, with much narrower intervals through the observations than the initial prediction. The MLE and MAP means are close in the observed region. MAP's larger measurement variance leaves more uncertainty there, and its shorter lengthscale lets uncertainty grow faster beyond the observations.

    These are intervals for the underlying function, not for a future noisy measurement. They condition on the fitted hyperparameters and do not include uncertainty in those parameters.
